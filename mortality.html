How this tool works — in plain English

The question. You are considering funding a cluster-randomized study of a program's effect on all-cause child deaths. Before spending millions, you want to know: will it come back with an answer, or just an expensive shrug?

What you tell it. What you currently believe about the effect (a best guess and a range), how deadly the setting is, the shape of the study (clusters, children, follow-up, how much clusters differ), what it costs, and what funding decision rides on the result.

How it answers. The page walks the value calculation step by step so every number can be audited: the funding decision as it stands today (①), what the study would measure (②), the four ways the post-study call can go — including the ways noise misleads it (③), what better decisions are worth in units of value (④), what the study costs in those same units (⑤), and the size at which the next data point stops paying for itself (⑥). A conventional frequentist power calculation sits at the bottom (⑦) as a sense-check on the whole chain.

The one core idea. The information in a mortality study is its deaths, discounted for clustering. Children and clusters matter only through the number of deaths you expect to observe — that is why mortality studies are so much bigger than coverage surveys.

What it is not. It is a design-stage sizing aid using bell-curve approximations, not a trial statistical analysis plan. With few clusters or few expected deaths it flags its own limits on screen.

⑥ The best study size

Each extra cluster buys a little more decision value and costs a little more money. Marginal study dollars face the same test as grant dollars: grow the study while the next cluster is still at least bar-level cost-effective (card 5's bar, default 4×); stop where it drops below.

The marginal test, cluster by cluster: the cost-effectiveness of the next cluster (blue, in multiples of cash) against your bar (grey). Keep adding clusters while blue is above grey; the best size is where they cross.

The same story cumulatively: the study's total value minus its total cost, at every size. Run it at the peak; below zero, the study is not worth running at all.
